# memoir

## Growing Up in Brazil

Tania grew up in Brazil in a household where the future was something you negotiated with circumstance, not something you designed. She was a curious child — drawn to how things worked, to the logic underneath the surface of things. She remembers the first time she used the internet as a moment that stayed with her. Not the content, not what she looked up, but the feeling: that there was an entire world of information that could be navigated, queried, traversed. That the machine was not opaque. That if you asked it the right question, it answered.

The path into technology was not available to her in the way it might have been elsewhere. In Brazil, the university entrance system requires a student to name their field of study at the point of application, and that choice is constrained by exam scores and by what the people around you believe is appropriate. There was no one in Tania's world pushing her towards engineering or computer science. She was steered towards something broader, and she graduated with a BSBA 2:1 in Business Management.

She was not bitter about it. The degree taught her how organisations work — how decisions get made, how resources flow, how a room of people with competing interests eventually moves in one direction. What it could not teach her was the thing she had felt at that computer years earlier: that underneath every system, there was a structure she wanted to understand.

After graduation, she received an unusual offer — the opportunity to train as a commercial aircraft pilot. The training materials were entirely in English. Tania's English, at that point, was not good enough. She made a plan: six months in London to learn the language, then return and begin the pilot training. It was a practical decision with a fixed timeline. It did not stay fixed for long.

## Six Months That Became a Life

London in those early months was disorienting in the way that any city is when you arrive without a map — not just geographically, but culturally. Tania was learning English in real time, from conversations and mistakes and the particular humiliation of not being able to say what she meant. She was sharp and she knew it, and the gap between what she could think and what she could express was its own kind of pressure.

But something else was happening underneath the difficulty. London was not like the city she had left. It was a place where the future was not pre-assigned. Where someone could arrive without connections, without a name that opened doors, and still build something. She saw people doing it. She saw that the rules about who got to become what were different here — or at least, more available to be broken.

The six months ended and she went back to Brazil. The pilot training was still there, still waiting. She began accumulating the flight hours the qualification required. And it was there, in the air, that the plan quietly came apart. The cabin pressure changes with altitude — the compression on ascent, the decompression on descent — turned out to be something her body could not accommodate. After each flight it would take more than an hour for her to stabilise. The disorientation was not minor. It was not the kind of thing that improves with repetition. It was a physical constraint with no workaround, and she understood, with the clarity that comes from confronting something immovable, that this particular future was not going to happen.

She went back to London. The decision to stay — really stay, permanently — was not dramatic. It was the accumulation of a hundred small recognitions that had been building since the day she arrived: that London was a place where the future was not pre-assigned, where someone could arrive without connections and still build something worth building. The pilot training had been the plan. London had become the reason. She started working. She started, in the way that immigrants do, from the ground up.

## Ten Years of Running Things

The first work she found was in events. It was physically demanding, unglamorous, and required the ability to hold many things in your head simultaneously while appearing entirely composed to the people who hired you. Tania was good at it. Within a year she was supervising. Not long after, she was managing. She held her first formal management role at the National Union of Teachers, and from there a decade-long career in operational environments took shape.

That career widened faster than she had expected. Corporate meetings gave way to large-scale social events. Large-scale events gave way to football stadiums. And then: the London 2012 Olympics. Tania managed catering logistics across one of the most scrutinised events on the planet — a delivery operation where the margin for error was close to zero, the timelines were fixed by something larger than any individual organisation, and the consequences of failure were visible to the world. She coordinated vendors, ran briefings, made decisions in real time when plans collapsed under the weight of what the day actually brought.

What she was learning — though she would not have used this language then — was systems thinking. Every event was an interconnected system: client-facing, production, vendor, logistics. Each layer depended on the others. A failure at one node propagated through all of them. She learned to map the dependencies before the day started, to identify where the single points of failure were, and to build redundancy into the places that could not be allowed to break. She learned to lead teams of up to fifty people under conditions where clarity was a form of kindness, and confusion was a form of cruelty.

## The Vaccination Centre

The NHS work was different in kind from everything that had come before. Tania joined University College London Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust as an administrative lead for one of the largest COVID-19 vaccination centres in London. It was not a normal operational environment. It was a system being built under emergency conditions, serving a population that was frightened, moving at a pace that normal operational planning could not fully anticipate.

The work required the same skills she had spent a decade developing — coordination, communication, decision-making under pressure — but applied to something with stakes she had not encountered in events. The person on the other side of the desk was not a guest at a corporate dinner. They were a 78-year-old man who had not left his flat in nine months. They were an NHS worker finishing a night shift and coming in before going home to sleep. Every operational decision — how the queuing worked, how the appointments were managed, how the edge cases were handled — had a human outcome.

She was good at it. And she knew, working there during the lockdowns, that something in her was reaching its limit — not exhaustion, but saturation. She had built the operational career. She had taken it as far as the domain allowed. There was something else she had been meaning to go after for years, and the pandemic, which had taken so much from so many people, had given her the one thing she needed: time.

## The Decision to Code

Tania started learning to code in the evenings. Free platforms, YouTube tutorials, whatever she could find. She had no CS background, no formal exposure to programming, and a full-time job managing a vaccination centre during a public health emergency. None of that was the point. The point was that the feeling she had had at that computer in Brazil decades earlier — that the machine had a structure, that the structure could be learned — was still there. It had never left. It had just been waiting.

She moved through the introductory material faster than she expected. Not because it was easy, but because the thinking patterns required — breaking a problem into its components, identifying dependencies, reasoning about failure modes — were patterns she had been practising for ten years in a different domain. Operational management had been, without her knowing it, a decade of training in systems thinking.

She applied to 01Founders, a peer-learning software development programme that worked differently from traditional bootcamps. There were no lectures. Students taught each other. The difficulty was the point. The programme pushed into systems thinking, into security, into building real things and understanding why they broke. Tania got in.

## Finding the Right School

01Founders confirmed something she had suspected: that she did not want a surface-level introduction to programming. She wanted to understand the substrate — how memory worked, how networks moved data, why one architectural decision made a system brittle and another made it resilient. She wanted the version of software engineering that resembled what she had been doing in operations: seeing the whole system, not just her corner of it.

She earned Student of the Year. She completed the Founders and Coders bootcamp. She was offered a mentorship position at Capital City College Group, where she taught Python, Go, and JavaScript to over fifty students. Teaching was not a pause in her own development — it was an accelerant. The questions students asked forced her to explain things at the level of first principles, which meant understanding them at the level of first principles.

Not a single day has passed since she started without her writing code or working through a technical problem. It is not discipline. It is the same pull she felt at that first computer — the sense that there is a structure underneath things, and that understanding the structure is worth spending a life on.

## What She Carries Forward

The ten years in operations did not end when the software engineering began. They became the foundation it was built on.

Most developers acquire deadline discipline slowly, through missed releases and post-mortems. Tania arrived with it. Most developers learn to communicate under pressure over years of incidents and outages. Tania learned it managing catering logistics for the Olympics and coordinating a vaccination centre during a pandemic. Most developers eventually develop the instinct to look for single points of failure. Tania built that instinct as a survival skill before she wrote her first function.

The transition from operational management to software engineering was not a career change in the way that phrase usually means — a departure from one thing and an arrival at something unrelated. It was a translation. The domain changed. The discipline did not.

She builds the way she ran operations: end to end, with security and reliability as constraints that shape every decision, with an understanding that the thing she ships will be used by real people in conditions she cannot fully control, and with the accountability that comes from knowing the outcome is hers to own.

## Summary

Tania grew up in Brazil with an early and persistent pull towards technology, studied Business Management because the path to engineering was not available to her at the time, and came to London at twenty-something intending to stay six months and return to pilot training. She stayed. She built a decade-long career in high-stakes operational management — corporate events, football stadiums, catering logistics for the London 2012 Olympics, and an administrative lead role at UCL Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust during the COVID-19 pandemic, where she helped stand up one of London's largest vaccination centres. During the lockdowns, she began teaching herself to code. She found 01Founders, earned Student of the Year, completed Founders and Coders, and became a Technical Mentor teaching Python, Go, and JavaScript to over fifty students. The operational career was not a detour. The deadline discipline, the systems thinking, the pressure tolerance, the instinct to look for single points of failure — all of it carried forward into the engineering work. She builds end to end, with security and reliability as first principles, and takes full responsibility for the outcome.
